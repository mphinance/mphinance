# You're Not Weak. You're Standing in the Wrong Spot.

*Tags: acceptance, recovery, career, circle of competence, ADHD, finding your work, lemonade out of lemons*

I was reading a Brandon Sanderson book the other night, the one where the prince is training a bunch of green recruits, and one scene stopped me cold enough that I took a picture of my Kindle like a crazy person.

A young recruit named Zabra wants to fight on the front line. She sets her shield, sets her stance, does everything right. Then a random guy with no training and "unfair genetics" walks up and shoves her flat on her back without trying. She's furious. She's humiliated. She wants to storm off.

And here is what the prince does. He doesn't lie to her. He doesn't say "you've got heart, kid, you'll get there." He squats down next to her in the dirt and tells her the truth: if I put you on that front line, people die. Your friends die. You are not built for this spot.

Then he says the part that actually matters.

> "You can storm away now and tell yourself my stupidity has prevented your heroism. Or you can make another choice. You can run messages. Learn your way around the military. See if there's a spot for you."

That is the whole thing. That is the entire game, and almost nobody plays it right.

## The lemonade thing is only half true

You've heard the cliché your whole life. When life gives you lemons, make lemonade. Turn your weakness into a strength. Every bad thing is secretly a gift.

Here's the truth: that's half a lie, and the missing half is the only part that works.

The toxic version says your weakness is secretly a superpower. That if you just believe hard enough, the thing that keeps knocking you on your back is actually your hidden edge. That's garbage. Zabra getting flattened was not secretly her becoming a great duelist. She was never going to be a great duelist. That part was real. The genetics were real. The lack of training was real.

The acceptance came first. The prince made her look at the truth before he offered her a single other option. You don't get to the new spot by pretending you belong in the old one. You get there by accepting, fully and without flinching, that the old spot was never yours, and then asking the only useful question left: where does this exact set of traits actually become an asset?

That's not lemonade. That's repositioning. Same fruit, different field.

## My lowest-paying job taught me how money actually works

Accounts payable. That was the job. I sat there matching invoices to purchase orders for less money than almost any job I've had since, and on paper it was the bottom of the finance food chain.

It was also the most educational job of my life, and it is not close.

Because here is what you learn in AP that you will never learn from an income statement. You learn who actually pays their bills and who stalls for ninety days. You learn that the number on the financial statement and the number in the bank account are two different animals living in two different time zones. You learn how working capital really moves, which is to say it moves like a stalling tactic, slowly and on purpose. You learn that a CFO can make a quarter look however he wants by deciding which checks go out this week and which ones wait.

Every earnings report I've ever been skeptical of, every "the cash flow doesn't match the story" alarm bell I've ever heard, I learned to hear in that cheap chair. The low-status job was a backstage pass to how corporate America actually breathes. I just had to stop being mad it didn't pay well long enough to notice what it was teaching me.

## The traits I used to apologize for

Let me give you the rest of mine, because this is where it gets fun, and a little dark, which is on brand.

**ADHD.** A single chart of NQ futures bores me into a coma. One instrument, all day, watching the same ticks. Some people thrive on that. I would rather chew glass. For years I treated that as a discipline problem, like I just needed to white-knuckle my way into being a focused futures guy. I'm not a focused futures guy. So I trade equities, where there are thousands of names and a new story every day and my brain actually wakes up. I didn't fix the ADHD. I stopped standing in the spot that punishes it and found the spot that pays it.

**Knowing what I don't know.** I know semis. I've put in the reps, I trust my own judgment, and when I have a read on that sector I size up. I do not know healthcare. Biotech binary events make my head spin, and you know what I do about it? Nothing. I don't force it. I find smart people who live in that world and I listen to them. Buffett's partner Charlie Munger called this your circle of competence, and the entire trick is not the size of the circle. It's being honest about where the edge of it is. Accepting your ignorance out loud is an edge. Pretending you don't have any is how accounts blow up.

**Recovery, relapse, all of it.** I'm an open AA and NA member. I've got the felony, the character defects, the receipts. And I've relapsed. More than once. For a long time that was just a stack of evidence that I was broken.

Now look at it from the field instead of from the floor. I have relapsed and come back, which means I understand the mechanics of a relapse, the lying little voice and the slow slide and the morning after, better than almost any clean-cut counselor who read about it in a book. If you are going through one, I am not guessing. I've been in that exact dirt. That's not a disqualification from helping people. It's the qualification.

And here's the lemonade one, the one that makes people wince before they laugh. If I ever decide to formally advise people on their money, I am sitting on a steady stream of clients whose single biggest expense line just dropped off a cliff. People getting sober free up an astonishing amount of cash flow. They've also usually got financial wreckage to clean up and they trust someone who's been there over someone in a suit who hasn't. The thing the world wrote me off for is the exact thing that builds the trust. I'm not standing where they shamed me for standing. I moved to the spot where the same history is the whole point.

**The background itself.** I can't hide the felony, so I stopped trying, and radical transparency became the entire brand. Nobody can ever blackmail you with a secret you already published. The thing that was supposed to be my permanent liability is now the reason people believe me when I show real P&L and real screenshots instead of a fake highlight reel. Acceptance turned a wall into a door.

## Recovery was the original school for this

None of this is new. It's just the Serenity Prayer with a brokerage account.

God grant me the serenity to accept the things I cannot change. That's the front line you were never built for. The genetics, the wiring, the past, the boring chart. Stop body-slamming yourself into it.

The courage to change the things I can. That's running the messages. Learning your way around. Finding the spot.

And the wisdom to know the difference. That's the whole ballgame, and it is the part that takes the longest to get.

I spent years trying to be a different kind of person before I figured out the move was to be the most useful version of this one. Recovery taught me that you don't win by fixing every defect. You win by getting honest about which ones are permanent, and then standing somewhere they finally work for you instead of against you.

## So here's the actual exercise

Don't make a gratitude list. Make a repositioning list.

Take the thing you're most tempted to apologize for. The low-status job, the wiring you can't change, the gap in what you know, the part of your history you'd erase if you could. Write it down plainly, no spin, no pretending it's secretly a gift.

Then ask the prince's question. Not "how do I fix this," and definitely not "how is this secretly amazing." Just: where on the field does this exact thing stop being a wound and start being an asset?

The lout with no training still got knocked down plenty later in his own way. Zabra still couldn't duel. The weakness stayed a weakness. They just found the patch of ground where it didn't matter, and where everything that made it a liability over there made it a weapon over here.

You're probably not weak. You're probably just standing in the wrong spot, getting knocked down over and over, telling yourself somebody else's stupidity prevented your heroism.

Get up. Go find your spot on the field.

- Michael Hanko, Momentum Phinance
