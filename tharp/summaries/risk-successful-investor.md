# How to Use Risk to Become a More Successful Investor — Partial Summary

Source: `1 - How to Use Risk to Become a More Successful Investor by Dr. Van Tharp.pdf` (148 pages, scanned image PDF, OCR'd via `pdftoppm`+`tesseract`; only sampled, not fully extracted). This is Volume 1 of Van Tharp's 5-volume "Peak Performance Home Study Course" (same course referenced in `trading-excellence-model.md`) — Stress (Vol 2), Attitude (Vol 3), Discipline (Vol 4), and Sound Decisions (Vol 5) round out the set and were triaged and skipped (see note at bottom).

**Most of this volume is psychological self-assessment** (the "Investment Psychology Inventory" — WR/PC/SF/JH/CF/IN scored self-tests, commitment/mindset narrative, loss-aversion/prospect-theory discussion, "point of no return" psychology) and is skipped here as not transferable to an autonomous agent. **One chapter has real, quotable, quantitative mechanism** and is summarized in full below.

## Chapter VII — Techniques to Objectively Measure Risk (the one section worth keeping)

Opens with a real, useful framing: *"There is nothing wrong with taking a risk. The danger occurs when you don't recognize that you're taking one."* (quoting Harry Browne). Four objective ways to quantify how much risk you're actually running, given as Figure 7-1:

1. **Standard deviation of monthly % account returns over a 12-month period** — the book's primary recommended metric. Procedure: record account value at the close of each of the last 13 months, compute month-over-month % change (adjusting for any additions/withdrawals), then take the standard deviation of those 12 monthly % changes. Worked example given: a sample account had a standard deviation of 10.74; a second, more volatile example had 160.54.
   - **Real benchmark data cited**: 40 commodity funds, Feb 1983–Mar 1984, had a **median risk score (this same std-dev metric) of 7.0**. The 10 riskiest funds ranged 9.07–18.86 and included 3 of the 5 best performers *and* all 5 of the worst performers — i.e., high variance in returns correlates with landing at both extremes, not just with big gains. The 10 least-risky funds included 2 of the top 5 performers and *none* of the worst 5 — a real, cited data point for "lower variance doesn't mean lower returns, it means you avoid the worst outcomes," directly supporting the "same trades, different sizing" thesis with an independent, real fund-universe dataset rather than a backtest or simulation.
   - **A percentile-based risk tolerance ceiling table** (from the paired psychometric test, the IPI): Top 2% of test-takers → max tolerable std-dev of 30; Top 5% → 25; Top 10% → 20; Top 25% → 15; 25-50th percentile → 10; 50-75th → 5; Bottom 25% → 3. Explicit recommendation: **target about half of your personal maximum**, not the ceiling itself.
2. **Probability of success with 95% confidence limits** — treats your historical win rate as a sample estimate with a margin of error, not a fixed truth. Formula given: `95% CI = p ± 1.96 × √(p(1−p)/n)`, where p = observed win rate, n = number of trades (use 2.58 instead of 1.96 for a 99% interval). Worked example: 100 trades, 40% observed win rate → the true win rate is estimated to lie roughly between 30% and 50% at 95% confidence. **Direct, correct statistical practice** — flags that a modest trade sample can't pin down your real edge nearly as precisely as it feels like it can, which is the same "you don't actually know your true win rate" caution behind the half-Kelly recommendation found elsewhere in this research.
   - Explicit caution attached: test any system "over a wide variety of market conditions (5 to 10 years, as many trades as possible)," and never test on the same data period used to develop the system (in-sample vs. out-of-sample, stated plainly, decades before that terminology was standard vocabulary).
   - States a real, load-bearing rule directly: **"a success rate above 50% is not required to be successful if one follows a golden rule of investing: cut losses short and let profits run"** — i.e., low win-rate systems can still have strongly positive expectancy, provided the payoff asymmetry is large enough. Matches the expectancy math found in the Systems Development Workbook (e.g., System 2 in that book's practice exercises: 34% win rate, wins 123x the size of losses, still a strongly positive-expectancy system).
3. **Calibrating your prediction ability** — a self-test measuring the relationship between your *stated confidence* in a prediction and your *actual* hit rate at each confidence level ("calibration curve"). Referenced but not detailed in the sampled pages (points to Chapter VI and Appendix I).
4. **Risk of ruin** — cites Dr. Peter Griffin's (a gaming-theory expert) formula for risk of ruin, with the one takeaway stated in caps in the original: **"IF YOU ARE A NET LOSER, YOUR RISK OF RUIN IS 100% IF YOU CONTINUE TO USE THE SAME STRATEGY."** The formula's actual derivation is in an appendix not reached in this pass.

## Triaged and skipped, no file produced (all confirmed via table-of-contents sampling, not full reads)
- **Volume 2 (Stress)** — general/trading-specific stress, autonomic nervous system, Arousal Theory, General Adaptation Syndrome, Jacobson's Muscle Tension Model — pure physiology/psychology, no transferable mechanism.
- **Volume 3 (Attitude)** — "Understanding Losses," Hydraulic Model, Servo-Mechanism model of the trader, self-esteem/meaning-of-money exercises — pure psychology.
- **Volume 4 (Discipline)** — beliefs/mental strategies/mental states, NLP-style reframing techniques ("Meaning Reframe," "Context Reframe"), state management — pure psychology, no formulas.
- **Volume 5 (Sound Decisions)** — internal representation systems (sounds/pictures/feelings), the "TOTE Model" of decision strategy, "Convincer Strategy," decision-making problem taxonomy — pure NLP-flavored decision psychology, no quantitative content.

None of these four contain position sizing, risk, or system-design mechanism — confirmed by contents-page sampling before deciding to skip rather than skipped on title alone.

## Flagged for future agent integration
- The **95% confidence interval on win rate** is directly implementable: any strategy an agent tracks should carry a confidence band around its live win rate, not a point estimate, and that band should visibly narrow only as trade count grows — useful for deciding when a strategy has enough of a track record to size up.
- The **std-dev-of-monthly-returns risk score**, benchmarked against the real 40-fund dataset (median 7.0), could become a genuine "how hot is this strategy running" gauge for a dashboard — a single, real number with an external benchmark to compare against, rather than an internally-relative measure only.
