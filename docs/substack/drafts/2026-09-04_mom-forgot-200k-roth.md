# My Mom's $165-A-Month Gift From The IRS

*She swears she never signed up for the Roth 401(k). Half her account says otherwise. Here's how to check your own, two clicks deep in a tab nobody opens.*

![hero](hero_mom_roth.png)

There's a story that goes around every couple of years about Fidelity auditing which of their accounts performed the best. The answer, supposedly, was the dead ones. Second place went to the people who forgot they had an account at all.

Fidelity has never confirmed it. Nobody has ever produced the study. It gets repeated because it's too good to check, which is normally my cue to ignore a thing entirely.

Then I went through my mother's 401(k).

She's alive, for the record. She just wasn't paying attention for fifteen years, which the story says is nearly as good.

Mom, this one's for you. You're not going to love that I did the math on you.

She called to go over her rollover paperwork and told me, for at least the third time, that she never did the Roth option at work. She was pretty sure about it. Then we opened the statement and went line by line.

The account is about **$400,000**. Half of it is Roth.

She wasn't lying to me. She had no idea. That money went in at a job she left fifteen years ago, hasn't been touched since, and has been sitting there quietly doubling under a label nobody ever reads.

## Why she didn't know

A 401(k) statement shows you a balance and a fund lineup. That's the page they want you looking at, so that's the page you look at.

The money source, whether each dollar went in pre-tax or Roth, lives in a separate breakdown two clicks deep called something like "contribution sources" or "money types." She never clicked it. I've been helping with her accounts for years and I never clicked it either.

The only time anybody actually audits a 401(k) is during a rollover, because the receiving custodian has to know what kind of money it's taking. That's the one moment the plan is forced to tell you the truth in plain language.

So go click it. Today, on your own plan and your spouse's. Took us fifteen minutes. It found $200,000.

## Part one: the years she paid for it

Working backwards from the balance. Fifteen years untouched at 7% is a 2.76x, so the account was around **$71,629** the day she stopped adding to it. Getting there takes about **$300 a month for twelve and a half years.**

`$300 x [(1.00583^150 - 1) / 0.00583] = $71,629`

So she put in **$45,000** of real dollars, three hundred at a time, out of paychecks that weren't big at the time.

Now here's what choosing the Roth actually cost her, because I had this wrong when I started. **The $300 wasn't the cost.** That $300 was going in either way. The only difference is that Roth money gets taxed on the way in, so at a 25% rate, $300 into the Roth cost her **$300** of take-home while $300 into the regular 401(k) would have cost her **$225**.

The choice cost her **$75 a month**, paid $75 at a time, and she doesn't remember a single one of them leaving.

## Part two: the fifteen years she did nothing

Then she left that job and forgot the account existed.

She didn't add a dollar, didn't rebalance, didn't panic-sell in March of 2020, mostly because she didn't know there was anything to panic about.

`$71,629 x 1.07^15 = $197,626`

**$125,998 of growth, produced entirely by not touching anything.** Almost three times what she put in.

I do this for a living and I can't tell you the last time I left something alone for fifteen years. I check positions before I've had coffee. I pay monthly for software that tells me things about accounts I was never going to touch anyway. She beat me at my own job by losing a login.

## Part three: what that $75 a month buys her now

Say she starts pulling the standard 4% a year off it.

**$197,626 x 4% = $7,905 a year. That's $659 a month, tax-free, all of it.** No withholding, no quarterly estimate, nothing that shows up on a return.

Now run the other version. Same $300 a month, same twelve years, same fifteen years of ignoring it, but into the regular pre-tax 401(k). Identical balance, **$197,626**, because the contribution was identical. She takes the same 4%, the same $7,905, and hands 25% of it to the IRS.

**$5,929 a year. $494 a month.**

So the trade she made without noticing was this. **She gave up $75 a month for twelve years and bought herself $165 a month for the rest of her life.**

More than double the money back, every month, and it never gets taxed. Draw for 25 years and the gap comes to **$49,407**.

It grows, too. Pulling 4% off something compounding at 7% means the balance still climbs about 3% a year, so the paycheck climbs with it. In ten years that same 4% is **$885 a month**. In twenty it's **$1,190 a month**.

That's the closest thing to a money glitch I've ever seen in a real person's account, and it ran for fifteen years with nobody watching it. She still swears she never signed up.

## Where she got lucky, and I won't

She wasn't making much at that job, so she paid her tax at the bottom of her career. Then her income climbed in a slow boring straight line for twenty more years.

Mine doesn't do that. Mine has really big highs and really big lows and it looks more like a chart than a career. Same rules, opposite answer. For a straight-line earner, front-load the Roth, because your lowest bracket is behind you and it's not coming back. For anybody whose income swings, the bracket is the signal: go pre-tax in the blowout years when you're shielding money from your top rate, convert in the lean years when it's cheap. Most people just white-knuckle a down year. It's the only time that conversion goes on sale.

I run both, because I never know which kind of year is coming.

And hold the tax rate flat and none of it matters anyway. If she'd gone pre-tax and invested every dollar of the $75 she saved, she'd have landed in exactly the same spot. Nobody invests the difference. Nobody. The Roth wins because it locks the tax savings somewhere your hands can't reach.

## Three things I still have to get right on the other half

The Roth half is done. It won already and it needs nothing from me. The **$200,000 pre-tax half** is where the work is, and I had two of these backwards.

**The conversion window closes at 73.** I'd been thinking of required distributions as money we'd move into the Roth later. You can't convert an RMD. Once they start, the required amount comes out first and it's taxable, and only what's above it is eligible. The first RMD on $200,000 runs around **$7,500**. That deadline has an actual date on it.

**The five-year clock doesn't travel.** Rolling a Roth 401(k) into a Roth IRA doesn't bring the 401(k)'s years with it. The receiving IRA's own clock governs the earnings. Her existing Roth IRA is well past five years, so it covers the whole $200,000 landing on top of it. A $15,000 account she barely remembers opening just saved the clock on $126,000 of growth.

**And the dumb one.** I said I'd load the pre-tax side with high dividend names so there's cash on hand for the RMDs. Backwards. An RMD is a percentage of the December 31st balance and has nothing to do with what the account throws off. Dividends don't reduce it, don't satisfy it, don't change it by a dollar, and you can take the distribution in kind without selling anything. Reaching for yield to "cover the RMD" is a yield chase in a costume, and I almost did it on my own mother's money because it sounded responsible.

What I'm actually going to do is the thing I do with my own book. Sell calls against the shares in the pre-tax half. Same $7,500 problem, except now the cash shows up because somebody paid me for time instead of because I reached for a yield I didn't believe in, and I never have to sell a share I wanted to keep. Being the patient one on the other side of somebody else's lottery ticket is the only edge I've got. It might as well fund my mother's RMD.
