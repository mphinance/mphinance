# [FABLE-VOICE] Silver Ran 15%. My Strike Never Hit. I Made 57.6%.

*Options 50% | Trading 30% | Mindset 20%*

Chad (who reads this thing, and who built the intraday reversals guide I published here) looked at all the tooling I'd been showing off and dared me to actually point it at something and make real money. Actual money. The kind with a comma in it.

I didn't have the cash for that and I didn't have the time. What I did have was $232 sitting in the only account I'm approved for options in (not a flex), and a plane ticket. So I did the realistic version of the dare instead - put $132 on one idea, and write down how I was getting out before I ever got in.

That second part is going to matter a whole lot more than it sounds like it should. But we'll get to that.

Twenty days later I closed it at +57.6%. That's 76/132, which is also seventy six dollars (nobody is buying a boat). Silver went up more than 15% while I held it, my strike never traded in the money for a single minute, and we still managed to squeak out some good profits. Somehow!

## The screen, and the trap sitting inside it

The plan was the dumb one, so we tried to run it as intelligently as a dumb plan allows. Find the cheapest far out-of-the-money calls the unusual-flow screen can cough up, buy a fistful, pray (the classic). The screen itself is honest enough - when today's volume on a deep OTM contract is bigger than its open interest, somebody is opening a fresh bet rather than closing an old one. Fresh lotto tickets, flagged automatically.

Then we priced every candidate through an edge model (it measures each contract's implied volatility against a fair-value surface built from the rest of that chain), and the whole picture flipped. Nearly every crowded, cheap, far-OTM call came back tagged very_rich, edge direction SELL. The 42 cent lotto with 11,000 contracts of open interest that everybody piles into? That's the one the pros are perfectly happy to keep selling you.

That's the structural reason the "just buy cheap calls" gospel is bullsh*t. You're not finding a mispricing. You're paying the premium the other side of the trade is thrilled to collect. A flow screener does not hand you a trade - it hands you a crowd, ya sillies.

One name came back where the flow, the edge model, the liquidity and the price all agreed: the silver ETF, August 21 expiry, the $62 strike. Positive modeled edge, roughly 8% spreads, 12,700 open interest with real volume trading on it, and it cost 65 cents.

## What I actually paid

Three lots across two mornings. Note lot 2. I paid a penny more thirteen minutes after lot 1, on a tape that was actively falling, which is exactly the sort of thing I'd flag if you sent it to me (and I would be insufferable about it, too). It's a $47 ticket so nobody died, but it raised my basis on chasing. Which is a dumb reason. Lot 3 the next morning at 0.39 pulled the average back down: (46 + 47 + 39) / 3 = $44 a contract, or 0.44.

Final position - 3 contracts, $132 all in, strike sitting 19.1% out of the money with silver at 52.06.

## Then the hole

Silver bottomed at 51.19 on the 28th and the contract traded down to 0.15. My $132 was worth $45. Down 66%. Fun! We almost sold out, but I didn't see enough conviction from the bears to shove it all the way through.

I had 0 or 100% in mind going into this trade anyway, and since I didn't see enough to turn my back on it, it was cheek-clenching time (a technical term).

## The exits

On July 27, before any of it worked, we wrote the ladder down. Sell one at 0.88 for a double (that alone recovers two thirds of the basis), sell one at 1.32 for a triple, let the last one run. That decision got made on a Sunday, by the version of me with no money on the line in that particular minute. I like that guy.

Then I had to go on vacation, so we needed to improvise and protect a little, while maintaining the integrity of the "yolo".

August 7. I was leaving. A 0.88 limit on two contracts had worked all day, the ask never got past 0.71, and I was not about to fly out holding three lottery tickets I couldn't watch, so I re-priced and sold 2 at 0.60 seventeen minutes before the bell. Pure cover-your-ass. Booked +$32, pulled $120 of my $132 back out, and made the last contract functionally free.

Then I set a good-til-cancelled order on the last one at 100% and got on a plane.

I want to be precise about that, because it would be very easy to write this paragraph as discipline. It wasn't! SLV closed 57.50 that day, its first confirmed close above the 56.37 resistance, and I sold into that breakout for reasons that had nothing to do with the breakout. My edge was a departure gate.

August 10. The contract printed 1.17 at 3:25pm. That was the top tick of the whole trade, and I was not at a screen. Had I been, I'd like to think I'd have taken it. Cute, right? (I'd like to think a lot of things.) I've watched myself long enough to know what actually happens - 1.17 becomes "it's going to 1.30," and 1.30 becomes 0.30.

August 12. Silver gapped up and my resting order filled at 0.88 at 9:51am, exactly +100%, without me. The mark fifty minutes later was 0.895 and the day faded from there. I got filled within a penny and a half of that morning's high, by a limit order I'd typed four days earlier and then completely forgotten about in another time zone. The best trader in this story is an order I forgot existed.

$132 in, $208 out, +$76, twenty calendar days.

## The part that should bother you

That same 62 call is worth 30 cents as I write this, two days from expiry, with silver at 60.01. It needs 62.44 by Friday just to break even, and it's currently pinned to a $49 million net gamma wall at the $60 strike in a regime that specifically damps moves like that (translation: pinned means pinned). Those contracts are going to zero.

Holding all three would have left me $90. Selling them got me $208. The exits were worth $118 more than simply being right.

And I was right! Silver ran from 52.06 to just over 60 while I owned this thing. A 15% move in a metals ETF in four weeks is enormous. The 62 strike never traded in the money once. Not for a minute. Not on the best day.

Direction was correct and direction was not sufficient. When you buy a strike 19% away with 29 days on it, you never get paid for being right - you get paid for premium expansion, and premium expansion is something that happens to you briefly and then leaves.

## Now the budget problem

On a 30-day chain, on a stock that trades between $90 and $800, every single contract priced under $70 is 25 to 30% out of the money. INTC's only sub-$70 call is 29% out. MSTR's is worse (of course it is).

So a $200 account doesn't get to choose the good option. The budget marches you straight into the far-out-of-the-money lottery ticket that the edge model says to sell, and then you tell yourself you picked it. I didn't pick the SLV 62 strike because it was good. I picked it because it was 65 cents.

The way out isn't a better strike. It's a cheaper stock. Slightly out of the money on an $18 stock costs the same as hopelessly out of the money on a $95 one.

## The one that passes

SOFI, September 18 expiry, $20 calls, 44 cents. Four contracts, $180 all in.

Edge model: very_cheap, direction BUY, execution score 99, slippage 1.12%, modeled edge after costs of $182 a contract. That's the best execution on anything I priced tonight, INTC included. It sits 8.6% out of the money against SLV's 19.1%, with a 0.308 delta against roughly 0.10 - it actually moves when the stock moves. There's 38,038 open interest at that strike, so it's liquid enough to get out of in a hurry. And cumulative delta across the day was +205,859 on 32,183 contracts, the second-strongest bullish flow on the entire board.

Now look one strike higher. The 22 calls have 148,852 open interest at 15 cents. That's where the crowd is (hi, July me). That's the ticket that fits everybody's budget, and it's the one I would have bought a month ago. The 20 strike is one strike less greedy, and it's the one the model actually pays for.

And it doesn't need a new high. SOFI's 90-day high is 20.13. I priced the contract at that level two weeks out and it comes to about 86 cents, which is essentially my first target. The trade works if the stock just goes back to where it already was this quarter. The SLV trade needed 62 on something that topped at 60. Big difference.

Better than that, it already got tested. SOFI closed at 17.66 on Monday, right on the shelf, and today it turned and ran +4.3% off a 17.84 low. So this isn't a line I drew on a chart because I wanted it to be there. It's a level the market checked one session ago and respected.

Which means being wrong is cheap, and it's obvious. A daily close under 17.55 and there's nothing left to argue about.

Two things I don't love and won't hide. Today's +4.3% came on 0.9x relative volume, which is a strong move nobody showed up for. And ADX is 14, meaning there's no trend here at all, just compression. That cuts both ways - the squeeze ratio is 0.82, which is coiled, and coiled things resolve in whichever direction they feel like.

That's the entire argument for a resting order over a market order, and it's the exact opposite of what I did on lot 2 in July when I chased a falling tape for a penny more. Sitting still is a skill. I do not have it naturally.

All three targets go in as resting GTC orders the day the entry fills. Not "watched for". Not "I'll see how it acts." The SLV trade made money because both exits were sitting in the book before I needed them.

Four contracts instead of three, for one reason - selling two at 0.90 returns $180 against a $180 basis, which is the whole thing back.
