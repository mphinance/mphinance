# Why I Don't Hide Any of It

*Momentum Phinance — March 2026*

---

People ask me why I'm so open about my stuff. The felony. The addiction. All of it. Right there on my Substack, on my trading platform, in conversation. Not hidden, not softened, not "I had some struggles in my past." The whole thing.

And honestly, until this week I didn't fully understand the answer myself.

## April 27, 2021

I didn't know the date until recently. That's when Chelsea Clinton's podcast dropped an episode called "Substance Use Disorders and Addiction" featuring Dax Shepard. I never would have picked that up on purpose. I don't even remember how it ended up in my ears.

But I know where I was when I heard it.

I was mowing lawns on a work crew, earning extra time off a jail sentence. I'd be sentenced the first week of May. A few days from finding out exactly how much of my life a judge was going to take. And I'm pushing a mower in the heat with headphones in, listening to Dax Shepard talk about being an alcoholic.

> 📸 **Image prompt:** *A lone figure pushing a lawn mower across a sun-bleached field, earbuds in, golden hour light, slightly overexposed, documentary style photography*

## The Part That Wrecked Me

Dax is talking about his kids. His oldest daughter was about three. He's heading out the door to his AA meeting and she asks where he's going.

"I'm going to AA."

"Why do you have to go?"

"Because I'm an alcoholic, and if I don't go there, then I'll drink and then I'll be a terrible dad."

"Can I go?"

"Well, no. You gotta be an alcoholic."

"I'm going to be an alcoholic."

And Dax says, "You know, you might become one. The odds are not in your favor. But you're not there yet."

I'm standing behind a lawn mower at a county work site, and I'm crying.

Because that little girl didn't hear "alcoholic" and think "broken." She didn't hear "disease" and think "weak." She heard her dad say he goes somewhere to get better, and she wanted to go too. She wanted to be one of them. Because to her, being an alcoholic just meant you were someone who showed up.

That's what honesty looks like when you don't put shame on it.

> 📸 **Image prompt:** *A father holding hands with a small child walking toward an open doorway filled with warm light, shot from behind, soft focus, film grain*

## The Thing About Making Excuses

I used to make up excuses all the time. At work, if I had to leave for a meeting, it was always "a doctor's appointment" or "I gotta take care of something." You learn to be creative about it. You get good at it. And then the lying itself becomes its own kind of weight, because now you're managing a whole second version of your life that doesn't exist.

Dax talked about this. He said when he relapsed after sixteen years sober, the hardest part wasn't the pills. It was lying to the people he loved. He said he could feel himself gaslighting his wife, telling her his symptoms were from a back flare-up, and the one thing sixteen years of sobriety had given him was the inability to do that anymore. He couldn't stomach it.

I get that. The exhaustion of the lie is worse than whatever people think of you when they hear the truth.

## Shame vs. Guilt

Dax said something I've carried ever since and I use it all the time:

**Guilt is "I did something bad." Shame is "I am bad."**

Guilt is useful. It motivates apologies, amends, change. Shame just tells you you're not worthy of love. Nothing constructive comes out of shame.

I lived in shame for years. I was a felon. I was an addict. I was the guy who couldn't get his shit together. And shame kept me right there, because if you believe you ARE the problem, why would you ever try to fix anything?

At some point I just stopped. Not because I got brave. Because I got tired. It's so much easier to just be honest. For me, at least, it's easier. I don't have to remember which version of myself I told which person. I don't have to make up a doctor's appointment to go to a meeting. I don't have to explain gaps in my resume with creative fiction.

I just say: I have a felony, I'm in recovery, and I build trading tools. All three are true at the same time.

> 📸 **Image prompt:** *A cracked mirror reflecting multiple versions of the same person, one in work clothes, one at a computer with trading charts, one sitting in a circle of folding chairs, moody lighting, conceptual photography*

## My Kid

I'm not going to hide it from my daughter either. Not because I'm trying to be some parenting guru. Because Dax showed me what it looks like when you don't hide it, and his three-year-old didn't think less of him. She wanted to go with him.

If I tell my kid the truth about who I am and what I've been through, and she grows up thinking that honesty is normal and getting help is normal, then maybe she never has to learn the hard way that lies are heavier than the truth.

That's not a guarantee. But it's a better shot than pretending.

## Step 12

In recovery, the twelfth step is about carrying the message. You take what you've learned and you share it with people who might need to hear it.

I don't know if this Substack counts. I don't know if some guy mowing lawns before his sentencing is going to stumble across this the way I stumbled across Dax's episode. Probably not. But maybe someone reads this and thinks, "okay, maybe I don't have to hide it either." Maybe that's enough.

Dax's friend told him something when he was scared to admit his relapse publicly: "If your goal is actually to help people, it's so much more helpful that you relapsed than it is you being sixteen years sober and married to Kristen Bell. That's not relatable. But lying to the people you love? That's relatable to almost everyone."

He's right. The polished version of recovery doesn't help anyone. The real one does.
