# The Beast Was Never the Problem

*Sandlot kids, the raise I waited eight months for, and the trade you keep not taking.*

Watched The Sandlot again with my kid.

If you've somehow missed it: kids hit a baseball signed by Babe Ruth over the fence into the yard with The Beast, a giant dog they're convinced will eat them. They spend the entire movie building Rube Goldberg contraptions to get the ball back without ever walking up to the front door.

Erector-set claw arm. Pulley system. Tunnel from the alley. Vacuum cleaner with a hook taped to the end. One kid almost gets eaten. Another one almost dies.

Eventually Benny just knocks on the door.

Mr. Mertle, the owner, turns out to be blind, kind, and a former Negro Leagues player who *personally knew* Babe Ruth. He gives them a different signed ball, sends them home, and ends up being the most important adult relationship in their summer.

Two hours of plot they could have skipped with one knock.

I sat there with my kid and thought about every time I've been those kids.

The raise I waited eight months to ask for, then got in a five-minute conversation. The girl I built a whole campaign around in college instead of walking over. The mentor I followed for a year before I sent a DM, who replied in three minutes and ended up shaping how I think about markets.

Every single one of those was a kid building an Erector-set claw arm for a ball he could have asked for.

And the most expensive version of this lives on my screen.

The setup is right there. The levels are clean, the convergence is clean, the screener has been screaming for three days. And I sit. I want one more candle. I want the volume to confirm. I want the news, the print, the tape, somebody else's blessing. By the time I "decide," the trade's gone and I'm either chasing it at a worse price or watching it print without me.

Same script as the kids. Same script as the raise. The Beast was never the problem. The not-deciding was.

The math on this is wild when you actually run it.

In life: the downside of asking is somebody says no. That's it. They don't punch you. They don't blacklist you. They don't tell the village. You go back to exactly where you already were, which is the same place you'd be if you never asked.

In a trade: the downside is your stop. A pre-defined number you already chose, in advance, on purpose. The upside is the move you saw coming three days before anybody else did.

We trade unlimited imaginary downside ("what if they think I'm pushy, what if I'm wrong, what if I look stupid") for a real downside that's bounded and small. We do it for so long that the not-asking and the not-deciding start to feel like the safe move.

They are not the safe move.

The safe move is the four words. The safe move is the entry at the level you already wrote on the chart in Sharpie last week.

I'm not great at this, to be clear. I'm writing this *to* myself as much as anyone reading it. I still sit on asks for weeks. I still pass on setups I waited a month for because the candle didn't close pretty enough.

But I'm trying to teach my kid the thing it took me until my forties to start doing: the fifteen seconds of fear between you and the decision is the whole game. Everything you want is on the other side of those fifteen seconds. Not all of it. Most of it.

Worst they can say is no.

Worst the chart can do is hit your stop.

Sometimes they say yes, and Babe Ruth's ghost personally vouches for you.

\- Michael
