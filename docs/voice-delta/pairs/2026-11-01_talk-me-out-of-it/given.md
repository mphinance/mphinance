## The tape first

Price first, the way Michael reads a tape. On Friday 10/30 the S&P fell **1.8%** and the Nasdaq **2.9%**. Intel had its worst day in decades after its guidance, and roughly **$1 trillion** came out of chip stocks in one session.

Two days earlier the Fed held rates, but the vote was **10 to 2**, and both dissenters wanted a hike. Next meeting is **Dec 8-9** (confirmed on the Fed's own calendar). Several outlets now have the market leaning toward a December hike.

<span style="color: red">**Rising-rate fear plus an AI-spending scare is a bad month to own ten names that all go down on the same day.**</span>

## How I argued with him

One agent saying "looks good" is a mirror. So I split it up:

1. Three agents pulled technicals, options positioning and two weeks of news on every name.
2. The survivors went through **three rounds** of bull vs bear, each round with a different lens: price and dealer positioning, then fundamentals and macro, then how you'd actually build the trade.
3. A separate judge read all of it, rechecked the contested numbers itself, and scored each name 1 to 10.

Twenty-eight agents by the end. One rule the whole way: every earnings or Fed date had to come from the company or the Fed, not a calendar site.

Then Michael changed the brief halfway through:

> "Not selling puts on most either shares or long calls"

He's a wheeler. Selling puts is his whole thing. The first pass graded everything as a put-selling candidate, so a few verdicts flipped once the question became "would you buy this?" PCG is the cleanest example: a fine name to sell puts on, a bad name to buy.

## The diversification problem

He said "I tried to diversify." Here's what ten names actually looked like once I ran the correlations:

![](fig_corr.png)

!> A correlation of **1.0** means two stocks move as one. Under **0.3**, they mostly ignore each other. Negative means one tends to zig when the other zags.

**NVDA and TXN at 0.51** is the only pair that really moves together, so they share one risk budget. The original list had three chip names and three rate-sensitive names. When chips and rates both went wrong Friday, six of his ten moved the same direction.

The two names I'm giving away free are the two that move least like everything else. NFLX runs **-0.33** against NVDA and **-0.39** against TXN.

## Free name 1: NFLX (5/10)

![](NFLX.png)

He asked me to check it "for craps and giggles" because it didn't fall Friday. NFLX closed **+0.05%** on a day the S&P lost 1.8%.

It also isn't reversing yet:

- Down **51%** from its high, below every moving average, all of them pointing down.
- A falling trendline from the September highs sits around **$68-69**. That break is the setup he likes best, and it hasn't happened.
- Q3 cut the ad revenue target from **$3B** to **$2.7B**, and Needham downgraded it to Hold after.
- Dealer positioning: a **$60** put wall under it, a **$65** call wall above, and a gamma flip around **$64.40-66**. Below the flip, moves get amplified.

The case for it: options are cheap (IV rank about **20**), which is the one time a premium seller should be the buyer instead, no earnings until late January, and it's the closest thing to a chip offset in the free pair.

> **Trigger:** a daily close over **$64** for a quarter-size position, add on a close over **$66**.
> **Stop:** **$60.90**. **Targets:** **$72.70**, then **$81-83.60**.
> **How:** a January $65 call, bought on trigger day only.
> **Deadline:** if neither trigger fires by **Fri 11/20**, drop it.

<span style="color: gold">**If the trigger never fires, it costs nothing.**</span> Buy the call today and sit flat for a month, and time decay eats about half of it.

The first research pass told Michael a new Stranger Things season drops Nov 26 and called it a catalyst. That was **2025**, and I'd already repeated it to him before the bull agent caught it in round two. Nothing like that is on the calendar this month.

## Free name 2: PYPL (4/10)

![](PYPL.png)

PYPL came out of a separate quality scan, and it's the lowest score that made the list.

- About **10x** earnings, with roughly **$9.4B** left on the buyback.
- A base of higher lows: **$50.80**, then about **$52**, then **$55**.
- Low beta (**0.58**), and low correlation to everything else here.

What capped the score: the CEO was replaced in February, PayPal's own checkout button isn't growing, and the August spike to **$62.58** was a buyout bid that died. Stripe and Advent walked away from **$60.50** a share on 8/28, and the stock gapped down 13% in one day.

So **$60** is the realistic ceiling, and it's only about 5% overhead. Any other month, Michael would sell the $55 put and get paid to wait right here. He said no puts, so it's a call spread. The bear ran the breakout trade and it came out slightly negative even with no edge assumed, because the target sits closer to the entry than the stop does. The dip trade survived:

> **Trigger:** a dip to **$55.00-55.50** that closes in the upper half of the day.
> **Stop:** **$53.40**. **Target:** **$60**.
> **How:** a January 55/60 call spread, about **$2.39** at Friday's prices. At $60 it's worth the full **$5** width: ($5.00 - $2.39) / $2.39 = **109%**.
> **Deadline:** no dip by **Fri 11/20**, no trade. No breakout entry.

## What a bad week looks like

Average the seven betas against the S&P, equal weight:

> (2.1 + 2.0 + 1.3 + 0.6 + 0.4 + 0.2 - 0.3) / 7 = **0.9**

So a **-3%** S&P week is roughly **-2.7%** for the basket, with NVDA and TXN doing most of the damage and EQT and NFLX cushioning it. The original ten would have taken more of that hit, because six of them moved together. That math holds in a normal bad week. In a real panic everything correlates, and nothing hedges anything.
